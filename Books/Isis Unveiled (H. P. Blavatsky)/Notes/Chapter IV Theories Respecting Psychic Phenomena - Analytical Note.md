## Argument (as she makes it)

The chapter maps rival *theories* of phenomena whose *facts*, she says, Catholics, Protestants, and (in part) academicians already concede ([[09 Chapter IV Theories Respecting Psychic Phenomena#^blavatsky-isis-v1-Page_99|pages 99–101]]). De Gasparin (Protestant) affirms levitations without contact “over thirty times” and denies spirits; des Mousseaux and de Mirville (Roman Catholic) write a “cyclopædic biography of the devil” as the motor of theurgy and modern manifestations. The church, she argues, would not so compromise herself over tricks she knew to be juggler’s work; she persecuted Paracelsus, Cagliostro, and Mesmer instead.

Des Mousseaux is quoted: “The Devil is the chief pillar of Faith,” and without “his speech which issued… from the mouth of the Serpent, *his medium*,” the Fall could not have occurred and the Cross would be “the most ridiculous of supernumeraries” (page 103). She reports this as the church’s echo, not as her own soteriology. The biblical idiom she uses of that devil, “a liar and murderer from the beginning” (page 101), matches [[John 008#^kjv-jhn-8-44|John 8:44]]; she does not print the verse number. De Mirville’s “commercial” God–Devil partnership and Goerres on apparitions as God’s chastening hand are likewise reported (pages 103–104).

Babinet’s muscular “little concordant impulsions,” ventriloquism, and the American “street-boy” origin-story are ridiculed (pages 104–106). She dwells on his “sphere-like lightning” that looked like a young cat in a tailor’s room (pages 106–108) to ask why science accepts one man’s meteor-cat and rejects thousands of Cideville witnesses. De Gasparin’s rule that certitude ceases when one “cross[es] the borders of the supernatural” is turned against both him and Babinet.

Will versus mediumship is stated more sharply than in Chapter II: a mesmerizer *wills* and, if strong enough, the thing is done; a medium succeeds the *less* he exercises will and must be “perfectly passive” (pages 109–110). Thury refuses departed spirits as demonstrated, refuses Gasparin’s “will of the experimenter” as sufficient, and (as Crookes reports him) posits a *psychode* and *ectenic* force like luminiferous ether. She identifies Thury’s ectenic force, Crookes’s psychic force, Éliphas Lévi’s astral or sidereal light, and Hindu *Akasa* as “identical,” “known to the gymnosophists… thousands of years ago” (pages 112–113). That identity is her claim. Thury, she adds, admits phenomena often produced by “wills *not* human,” a qualified nod to Crookes’s sixth (non-human beings) theory. After twenty years, she says, science has not stepped outside its “enchanted” materialist circle (pages 113–114).

A short philosophical burst defends immortality: Socrates and Cebes (Phaedo); “the seed cannot develop unless it is in part consumed” (Lecomte); “it is not quickened unless it die,” says St. Paul (page 114) — matching [[1 Corinthians 015#^kjv-1co-15-36|1 Corinthians 15:36]]. Pathologists who call believers lunatics are answered with “Physician, heal thyself” (page 117), matching [[Luke 004#^kjv-luk-4-23|Luke 4:23]]. The St. Petersburg Mendeleyeff commission and the Lankester–Slade case illustrate scientific prejudice (pages 117–118).

Catholic Lourdes “materializations” of the Virgin and American cabinet materializations of Mohammed or Joan of Arc are set in parallel: “Either both ‘miracles’ are possible, or both kinds… are arrant impostures” (pages 119–121). Monsignor Capel’s appeal to human testimony for Lourdes is paired with Huxley’s appeal to human historical evidence — she claims the same tribunal for psychological phenomena. This equalizing move is not a reading of unread Volume 2 chapters; it sits beside, without repeating, that volume’s preface against theological Christianity.

The close returns to comparative myth. Belief in supernaturalism is inherited from “the first races,” “the gods of their fathers, ‘that were on the other side of the flood’” (page 122; unnumbered biblical phrasing neighbouring Joshua 24). She quotes Genesis vi at length — men multiplying, sons of God taking daughters of men, giants — and the line “they bare children to them; the same became mighty men which were of old; men of renown” (footnote 223: Genesis vi. 4). Installed [[Genesis 006#^kjv-gen-6-1|Genesis 6:1]]–[[Genesis 006#^kjv-gen-6-4|6:4]] match those sentences. She sets beside them a Hindu story (via Polier) of the first Brahman given a wife of the *Daints* or giants, so that the priesthood descends from “superior spirits (the sons of God)” and a daughter of earthly giants; then the Scandinavian Bur/Bör/Besla fragment and Mexican *Popol-Vuh* races (pages 122–123). Shared “groundwork,” she says, not proof that any one narrative copied another in a demonstrated historical line — and not a repetition of Chapter I’s footnote 100 overlay about the flood’s purpose.

## Limits

Do not treat de Gasparin’s thirty levitations, the Cideville thunder-menagerie, or Babinet’s fire-cat as vault-verified events. Do not equate her *Akasa* identity with Gaskell’s LIGHT entry. Do not read the Lourdes parallelism as a digest of Volume 2.
